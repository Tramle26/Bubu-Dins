# Three real-life money decisions

**College · Try it · 3 minutes**

Choose what you would do, then open the explanation. A sentence per situation is enough.

## 1. Your paycheck has limits

Your campus job pays **$1,200 a month before tax**. After taxes, bills, and groceries,
only **$70 remains**. The book's 10% savings benchmark would be $120.

**Your move:** Would you start with $25, save all $70, or borrow to reach $120? Explain one trade-off.

<details>
<summary>Compare your decision</summary>

Starting with **$25** leaves $45 for surprises. Saving all $70 could work if you
already have emergency money, but leaves no monthly cushion. Borrowing just to hit
10% adds debt costs. Start with what fits and review after your pay or expenses change.

</details>

## 2. Tuition is due soon

You have **$800 for tuition due in eight weeks** and a separate **$100 for long-term
retirement savings**. A friend suggests putting both into a stock fund.

**Your move:** Which money needs a stable balance and easy access? Why give the two amounts different jobs?

<details>
<summary>Compare your decision</summary>

Keep the tuition money available for the bill: a stock drop could leave you short.
Retirement money has more time to ride out market swings, though losses are still
possible. The deadline matters more than simply being young.

</details>

## 3. Two similar funds, different fees

You are comparing two funds tracking the **same broad index**. On a $2,000 balance,
Fund A's 0.10% annual fee is about **$2**; Fund B's 1% fee is about **$20**.

**Your move:** Which would you investigate first, and what would you check before choosing?

<details>
<summary>Compare your decision</summary>

Start with **Fund A's lower cost**, then check other charges, what it owns, and
whether it fits your goal. The $18 difference stays invested in this simplified
one-year comparison. Lower fees do not guarantee a profit. A fund concentrated in
one industry would still carry concentration risk.

</details>
