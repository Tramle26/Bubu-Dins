# Seven small investing habits

**College · Learn · 4-minute read**

Part 2 of *First-Time Investor*, simplified for your first paycheck.

<bubu-coach>Start with what you can control: how much you save, what you pay, and when you need the money.</bubu-coach>

## 1. Choose the account, then the investment

An account is a **container**, not an investment. A taxable account can create tax bills along the way. A traditional IRA may give a deduction now, with tax later. A Roth IRA uses after-tax money; **qualified withdrawals are tax-free**. Eligibility and withdrawal rules apply.

## 2. Keep fees small

On $1,000, a **0.10% yearly fund fee is about $1**; a 1% fee is about $10. Compare similar investments: lower fees leave more money invested, but do not guarantee a profit.

## 3. Save an amount you can repeat

The book suggests **10% of income if you can**. On $1,200 monthly gross pay, that is $120. If your budget cannot support it, start smaller—perhaps $25—and review when your pay changes. Keep money available for bills and surprises.

## 4. Give each dollar a deadline

**Stocks** are company ownership; **bonds** are loans to companies or governments. Both can lose value. Stocks offer growth potential with bigger swings; bonds may soften those swings.

**Example:** Tuition due in eight weeks needs stability and access. Retirement money has decades to grow. Your age alone does not decide where every dollar goes.

## 5. Spread your investments

“Home sweet home” means your **home country's market**, not buying a house. Diversify across companies, industries, and countries. Three technology funds may own the same stocks. International investments broaden exposure but add risks, including currency changes. Diversification cannot prevent every loss.

## 6. Work backward from your future life

If future spending is $36,000 a year and other income covers $12,000, investments must fill a **$24,000 gap**. At an illustrative 4% initial withdrawal rate, the target is $600,000; at 3%, $800,000. All figures use today's purchasing power. These are planning estimates, not guaranteed safe withdrawal rates.

## 7. Let time do some work

**Compounding** means returns can earn more returns. Saving $5,000 at each year end for 40 years means contributing $200,000. At a hypothetical constant 7% return, it grows to about **$998,000**, before taxes, fees, and inflation.

The book's “$10 million” idea combines **retirement withdrawals plus money left to heirs** over a much longer timeline. It is not a promised retirement balance.
