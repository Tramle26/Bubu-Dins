## Investing decision practice

Your learning credit comes from saved lesson progress: Learn **$20**, Try it **$30**, and Review **$40**. It is practice money, not a real credit score or cash. These exercises do not spend it or award more credit. All firms, products, rates, and market changes below are fictional teaching examples.

## 1. A deadline or an opportunity?

You need **half of your earned credit for a course in 12 months**. The other half is for a goal at least ten years away. Assume you already have a separate emergency reserve and no expensive debt in this fictional case.

| Option | What the fictional bank tells you | What could go wrong? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bubu Easy Access savings | Illustrative 2% annual interest; withdraw anytime; no fee in this exercise. | The rate can change, and prices may grow faster than your savings. |
| Meadowbridge World Fund | Holds shares across many companies and countries; annual fund fee 0.20%; no promised return. | Its value can fall when you need the money. Selling takes time. |
| Lantern Cycle Works shares | One company seeking money to expand its bicycle workshop; no dividend promised. | Expansion may fail. You could lose your entire investment. A sale needs a buyer. |

**Make a plan using your actual learning credit below.** How much stays accessible, and how much would you consider investing? Keeping everything accessible is an option too.

<x-bubu-bank-budget />

Before deciding, explain: Which money can wait through a downturn? What evidence about Lantern's sales, debt, competitors, and expansion costs would you request? What would make you decline the investment?

<details><summary>Dr.Bos asks you to look again</summary>
A growing business can still be a risky investment. Compare the one-year deadline with the ten-year goal. A diversified fund spreads company risk, but can still lose value. Check withdrawal conditions and how quickly you could actually get your money.
</details>

## 2. The same market, a different price

A salesperson offers **Cedarvale Global Fund** at a **1.20% annual fee**, compared with Meadowbridge's **0.20%**. For this comparison only, assume both track the same index, hold the same investments, and have the same return before fees. Neither charges a trading fee in the example.

**On the fund amount in your plan, estimate each annual fee:** amount × 0.002 versus amount × 0.012. The difference is amount × 0.01. This is a simplified one-year calculation with a constant balance; actual fees vary with account value.

Would you pay the extra fee? What service or evidence would justify it? Does a higher price imply better future performance? What other costs or restrictions would you ask about?

<details><summary>Compare your reasoning</summary>
For an illustrative $30 fund allocation, the fees are $0.06 versus $0.36: a $0.30 difference for one year. Small fees can accumulate over time. A lower fee does not remove market risk or guarantee profit. Ask whether any additional service is useful and included in that price.
</details>

## 3. The market falls before your deadline

Six months later, imagine Meadowbridge is down **20%** and Lantern is down **40%**. These are stress-test assumptions, not forecasts. Ignore savings interest and fees for this calculation.

Your fund holding is now worth **80% of its starting amount**, and your Lantern shares **60%**. Your course payment is due in another six months.

Would you hold, sell some, or revise the goal? Explain which money you would use for the course, what selling would lock in, and why waiting does not guarantee recovery. What changes if your emergency reserve has also been used up?

<details><summary>There is more than one defensible answer</summary>
Separate a near-term obligation from money that can stay invested. Consider the cost of missing the course payment and whether your original plan took more risk than you could afford. A price drop alone does not tell you whether an investment will recover. Explain what new evidence would change your decision.
</details>
